Project purpose and business value

What Midas Studio does

It turns a supplier's product photo into standard catalogue images for Midas, the furniture retailer. The source is the Midas Kuwait English website, with Saudi Arabia and Qatar sites also referenced. For each product it makes:

  • a white master: the product alone on a pure white background, delivered as 1125 × 1125 pixel JPG and WebP files;
  • room scenes: the product in a styled room, using only real Midas accessories that are in stock;
  • a package: the files, a manifest (list of files and checks), a draft description and a cost record.

Sources: repo README.md, coverage register 15 Oct, stage 06 and 07 READMEs.

Who it serves

  • Intended users: the Midas in-house catalogue team, working from supplier photos.
  • Actual users today: the owner only, through an invite-only web app.
  • Clients: there is no live client path. The old client pipeline ("pipeline 3") is retired, and a client portal has not been built.

Sources: owner workspace DEPLOYMENT_STATUS.md; feature status 18 Oct; owner's answer on pipeline 3 (20 Oct).

The business problem

Catalogue images from suppliers vary in quality, background and styling. Making consistent white-background and room images by hand is slow and costly. The owner's documents describe the aim as "saving with higher capacity; cost per need, not per generation".

Source: coverage register section 1, citing the owner's source documents (not in the repo).

Intended benefits and what is measured

  • Intended 90–95% faithfulness to the real product. This is a target only; nothing measures it yet.
  • Measured once About USD 0.16 for one white-master run, and about 3 minutes of machine time plus owner waiting (20 Oct). These are estimates from usage, not invoices.
  • Not available Volumes, time saved compared with manual work, the current cost per image, or return on investment. Without these, the business case cannot be quantified yet.

Business processes from start to finish

4.1 Making images for one product (the main journey)

Starts with: the owner creating a project and uploading one or more supplier photos. Required information: the photo, what to make (white master and/or scenes), the piece count and name, and optional notes. Human decisions: two approvals, Go 1 and Go 2, plus one per scene. Nothing is released without the owner.

4.2 Editing an approved image

From an approved result the owner can ask for up to two changes from fixed lists: lighting, wall colour, floor, camera view, fewer or no props, or fixing a defect. The edit reuses the earlier photo reading and goes through Go 1 and Go 2 again. Built and published 19 Oct No real edit has completed. Five edit attempts on 19–20 Oct each stopped on a different fault; each fault was fixed in turn.

4.3 Keeping the accessory catalogue current

Scenes may only use Midas accessories that are in stock. A catalogue of 428 accessories was built from the Midas website (about 20% of the roughly 2,082 listed). A refresh workflow reads the website and updates stock. Ran 4 times Weekly schedule built but switched off. During each job the engine also checks live stock for the pieces it picks.

Automated tests use sample or code-made data. They prove the rules, not image quality. The 90–95% faithfulness target is not measured by any test.

Known limitations

  • The image model ignores the framing instruction on wide sets (97.6% of the width on 20 Oct, against a 72–80% target).
  • Colour can drift. The 20 Oct review failed on a warmer fabric tone.
  • Provider outages (HTTP 503) stopped runs on 16 and 20 Oct.
  • The photo reader was right on 9 of 10 test photos in its best test. That is a small sample.
  • Only 2 of 26 quality requirements are fully met; 14 are partial and 6 are pending.
  • No delivered output is marked fit for the catalogue.

Not verified

  • Room scenes, scene review, scene Go 2 and delivery files.
  • Any repair on a real job.
  • Any completed edit of an approved image.
  • The package step without manual recovery.
  • Costs against provider invoices.
  • Anyone other than the owner using the system.

Go 2 on the 20 Oct run: two readings

The review failed 2 of 16 checks: the fabric colour drifted, and the product touched the frame edge. The session had suggested a repair. The owner pressed approve, and the record reads "Owner approved · review failed". The owner does not recall the intent, so both readings are set out below.

A. A deliberate exception

The owner accepted the defects to see whether the package step would work on a real image. This was a test, not a catalogue release.

  • What it means: the system behaved as designed. It labelled the result truthfully as an owner exception, marked it not fit for the catalogue, and still held the package for the framing problem.
  • Business risk: low, as long as exception images never reach the catalogue.
  • What to do: keep it as a test record; repair or redo this product once the package fix is live.

B. A test click without reading the review

The messages around that minute ("checkgo", then "go" five seconds later) suggest the owner was moving the run along, not weighing the review.

  • What it means: the approval screen makes it easy to approve a failed image with one click.
  • Business risk: medium once more people use the system. Defective images could be approved by habit.
  • What to do: when a review fails, the approve button should ask for confirmation and a short reason, and suggest repair as the default.

Recommendation, in either case: treat the image as a test record, not a usable result, and add the confirmation step for approving a failed review (next action 6). That protects the catalogue whichever reading is true.
